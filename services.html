3 Core Stages of Craft

The Craft
Behind the Block.

Woodworking isn't a straight line from A to Z. It's a conversation between the craftsman and the grain—reading how the wood wants to move, where it'll stress, how it'll age. These three stages aren't just steps. They're where intent becomes permanence.

01 / Cutting

Precision Before
Everything Else.

Every joint starts with a clean, square cut. I mill raw lumber down to exact dimensions using a combination of table saws, planers, and jointers—removing warp, twist, and checking before the wood ever sees glue. Edge-grain? End-grain? The cut pattern dictates how that board will perform for the next fifty years.

What This Means for You

Zero gap tolerance on glue-ups—joints flush within 1/64"
Grain matching for visual continuity across large surfaces
Wood movement accounted for in every dimension
02 / Sanding

Hand-Finished,
Not Machine-Smooth.

Machine sanders are for production lines. I sand by hand through six progressive grits—starting at 80 to level the surface, ending at 320 for a finish that accepts oil or clearcoat without blotching. The goal isn't just smoothness. It's revealing the grain's natural depth without destroying the wood's character with over-sanding.

The Sanding Progression

Six-grit hand-sanding process (80, 120, 150, 180, 220, 320)
Beveled edges to prevent splintering over time
Grain direction followed on every pass for clarity
03 / Finish

Protection That
Doesn't Hide the Wood.

Finish is the final conversation with the grain. Clearcoat or oil—both seal the surface, but the choice depends on how the piece will live. Clearcoat for high-traffic countertops that need hard protection. Oil for cutting boards and serving pieces where you want the wood to breathe and age with use. Either way, I apply it in thin, controlled coats until the surface reaches a hand-rubbed depth.

Finish Options

Clearcoat: Water-based polyurethane, 3-coat minimum for durability
Oil Finish: Food-safe mineral oil + beeswax blend, hand-buffed
Curing time: 7-10 days for full hardness before delivery

The Wood
We Work With.

Not all hardwood is created equal. I source sustainably harvested timber with tight grain, minimal defects, and the structural integrity to last generations. Here's what ends up in the shop.

Acacia

Acacia melanoxylon
Dense Durable Rich Tone

Known for its deep, warm tones and exceptional hardness. Acacia's tight grain resists warping and holds up under heavy use—ideal for cutting boards and countertops that see daily action.

Shortleaf Acacia

Acacia holosericea
Fine Grain Smooth Stable

A lighter variant with tighter grain patterns and excellent dimensional stability. Perfect for edge-grain boards where you want visual consistency without heavy figure.

Hevea (Rubberwood)

Hevea brasiliensis
Sustainable Light Color Workable

Plantation-grown hardwood from retired rubber trees. Light blonde color with subtle grain. Machines beautifully and takes finish exceptionally well—great for contemporary, minimal aesthetics.

Chevron Mix

Multi-species parquetry
Geometric Contrasting Custom

Not a single species—a pattern. Chevron combines contrasting woods (walnut + maple, acacia + ash) in angled herringbone layouts. High labor, high impact. Every piece is a conversation starter.

Walnut

Juglans nigra
Premium Dark Grain Refined

The luxury standard. Deep chocolate tones with dramatic grain variation. Machines like butter, finishes like glass. Limited availability due to cost—reserved for high-end commissions.

Hard Maple

Acer saccharum
Classic Light Tone Rock-Hard

The workhorse. Cream-colored, dense, and nearly indestructible. End-grain maple butcher blocks are the commercial kitchen standard for a reason—they take abuse without complaint.
