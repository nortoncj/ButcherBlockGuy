How It's Made

Six Steps.
Zero Shortcuts.

Every piece moves through the same deliberate sequence — no stage skipped, no corner cut. This is what separates a board that lasts a season from one that lasts a lifetime.

01

Layout

Before a single tool is picked up, every project is laid out on paper and on the bench. Grain direction, wood pairing, visual flow — all planned by eye and hand. A good layout is the difference between a piece that looks intentional and one that just looks like wood.

02

Design

Form follows function — but it still has to be beautiful. Each piece is designed around how it will actually be used: the reach across a countertop, the weight of a dining table leaf, the grip on a cutting board edge. Design here isn't aesthetic. It's structural empathy.

03

Sizing

All stock is squared, planed, and cut to dimension in-house. We work to tolerances of 1/64" — because a joint that isn't flush isn't a joint, it's a gap. Every board, slab, and strip is measured twice and ripped once. That's not a cliché, it's shop law.

04

Cutting, Sanding & Shaping

This is where raw lumber becomes a piece. Rip cuts, cross cuts, curves, and profiles are worked on the table saw, band saw, and router table. Then sanding begins — five progressive grits from 60 through 220, moving with the grain, never against it. The surface doesn't lie: if it's not right here, nothing downstream fixes it.

05

Joining, Cutouts & Holes

Joinery is where craft separates from carpentry. Mortise-and-tenon, tongue-and-groove, dowel, and biscuit joints are chosen by application — not convenience. Sink cutouts, hardware holes, edge profiles, and insets are routed clean and square. Then it all goes together under clamp pressure until the glue cures fully — no rushing.

06

Conditioning & Putty

The final stage is where the wood comes alive. Open grain is filled with tinted wood putty matched to the species, sanded flush, and then the conditioning begins — multiple rounds of food-safe mineral oil or hardwax, applied by hand and buffed between coats. The result is a surface that's sealed deep, not just coated. Wood that feels the way it's supposed to feel.

"Putty tells the truth. If the grain is tight and the surface is honest, you barely need it. If you're reaching for it every five minutes, go back to step one."

— Troy Abraham
